For two lessons you have worked with one vector at a time. Real data does not arrive one at a time — it arrives as a table, one row per thing. This lesson is about doing something to every row at once, without a loop, and it ends with the search from 1.1 written the way a real system writes it.

A matrix is a stack of the vectors you already have

X = [[8, 7, 0],      ← "cooking"      one row = one thing
     [7, 9, 0],      ← "baking"
     [0, 0, 8],      ← "rust"
     [1, 0, 9]]      ← "python"

Not a new kind of object with new rules. What is new is doing something to all of them at once, and the whole lesson is the two tools that let you.

Shape is the thing you check

Every numpy array has a .shape: a tuple saying how big it is along each axis. For a table that is (rows, columns), written here as (n, d) — n things, each with d numbers.

One distinction matters more than it looks. All three of these hold three numbers, and they behave completely differently:

shape (3,)      a plain run of three numbers, no rows or columns
shape (1, 3)    one row of three
shape (3, 1)    three rows of one

axis: which direction do you collapse?

X.sum() adds everything and gives one number. Usually you want one number per row or one per column, and axis says which.

The rule that makes this stick: the axis you name is the one that disappears.

Broadcasting: the rule, and then the reason

You want to subtract three column averages from a (2, 3) table. Different shapes, and numpy does it anyway. The rule is shorter than its reputation:

  1. Line the shapes up from the right.
  2. Two dimensions are compatible if they are equal, or if one of them is 1.
  3. A shape that runs out on the left is treated as 1s.

keepdims, and the bug that says nothing

This is the sharpest edge in the lesson. You want every row of X scaled to length 1, and you have one length per row — shape (n,). Divide, and numpy lines that up against the columns.

Why this one gets a whole section

On a square table the bug is completely silent: the shapes are compatible, nothing raises, the answer is wrong. The tests for exercise 4 use a non-square table on purpose so numpy raises instead — but a real dataset will not do you that favour.

Transpose, and every dot product at once

X.T swaps rows and columns. (4, 3) becomes (3, 4), and what was at [i, j] is now at [j, i]. Nothing is computed; the same numbers are read the other way round.

A = [[1, 2],          A.T = [[1, 3, 5],
     [3, 4],                 [2, 4, 6]]
     [5, 6]]

shape (3, 2)                 shape (2, 3)

Now the other half. @ between two matrices pairs every row of the left with every column of the right, and each pairing is a dot product:

A = [[1, 2],     B = [[1, 0, 1],      A @ B = [[1,  4,  7],
     [3, 4],          [0, 2, 3]]               [3,  8, 15],
     [5, 6]]                                   [5, 12, 23]]

    (3, 2)     @     (2, 3)      ->            (3, 3)

Check one by hand: entry [0, 1] is row 0 of A against column 1 of B, which is 1×0 + 2×2 = 4.

Read the shapes. The inner numbers must match — they are the length of the vectors being dotted — and they vanish, leaving the outer ones. That is how you predict a matmul's shape without thinking about the arithmetic, and it is worth doing every time before you write the line.

Now put the two halves together. You want every row of X dotted with every row of X. @ pairs rows on the left with columns on the right. So what does the right-hand side need to be? That question is exercise 5, and the answer is one operation where a loop would have been n² of them.

A new axis out of nothing

Sometimes you need every pair of rows rather than every pair of dot products — every difference, so you can take every distance. X[:, None, :] inserts an empty axis. Read it as "every row, a new empty axis, every column".

At n = 2000 this builds a 2000 × 2000 × d array, which is worth knowing before you point it at a million rows. stretch.py measures both the speed and the memory, so the trade-off becomes a number rather than a warning.
