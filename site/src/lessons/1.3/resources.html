Watch and read

  • 3Blue1Brown, Essence of Linear Algebra — ch. 3, "Linear transformations and matrices" (~11 min). A warning, because it will feel like a mismatch: 3B1B treats a matrix as a movement of space, and this lesson treats it as a table of data. Both are true and they are not the same idea. Today you want the table. Watch it anyway — 1.4 is entirely the other view, and seeing it early makes that lesson land faster.
  • NumPy — Broadcasting (~15 min read). Official, short, good diagrams. Read it after the broadcasting section here, so you are checking your understanding rather than forming it. "General Broadcasting Rules" is the whole thing in six lines.

Reference, when you hit it

  • NumPy — Indexing on ndarrays. Go here when X[:, None, :] stops making sense. The section you want is "Dimensional indexing tools".
  • numpy.linalg.norm. Read the axis and keepdims parameters together — they are exercises 2 and 4 in one page.
  • numpy.argsort. Needed for exercise 7. Note which direction it sorts in, and what kind="stable" guarantees about ties.

After the exercises pass

  • 3Blue1Brown — ch. 4, "Matrix multiplication as composition" (~10 min). Only once exercise 5 passes. You have just written a matmul as "every pairwise dot product"; this says what @ means when neither side is data, and it is the bridge into 1.4.
  • Your own stretch.py output. The speed number and the memory number are the two facts that decide whether this approach survives real data, and neither means anything until measured on your machine.

Deliberately not yet

  • np.einsum. It does everything in this lesson in one call and is genuinely worth learning — later. Learn to predict shapes first; einsum rewards people who already can and punishes people who cannot.
  • Sparse matrices, BLAS, GPU arrays. The same ideas with different storage. Phase 5.
