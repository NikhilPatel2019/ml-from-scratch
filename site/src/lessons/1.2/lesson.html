In 1.1 you built a search engine and it worked. Here is what I did not tell you: there is more than one way to measure "closest", and they do not always agree — not in an edge case, but routinely, on ordinary data, in a way that changes which document your system returns.

Two questions, not one

Take two vectors. There are two completely different things you might mean by "how close are these?"

How far apart are they? Stand at one and walk to the other. That is distance, and it is Pythagoras again — the same formula as magnitude, applied to the gap between the two points instead of to one point.

distance(a, b)  =  |a - b|

Read it right to left: subtract to get the vector from b to a, then take its length. You already have magnitude. Distance is magnitude of the difference, and that is the whole of exercises 1 and 2.

Do they point the same way? Ignore how long each one is; ask only about direction. That is the angle, and cosine similarity — which you already wrote — is one step away from it.

Two vectors can be far apart and point identically. Two vectors can be close together and point in different directions. Neither measure is wrong. They answer different questions.

Where they disagree

Say documents are represented by two numbers: how much they talk about cooking, and how much they talk about baking.

Which one do you want?

  • Text, embeddings, recommendations — anything where "length" means "amount of document" rather than "amount of the thing" — use the angle. A long article about cats and a short article about cats are about the same thing.
  • Positions, coordinates, physical measurements — anything where magnitude is the signal — use distance. Readings of 20°C and 200°C are not "the same, just louder".

There is a shortcut worth knowing. If every vector is normalised to length 1, the two measures rank identically. They are not equal — still different numbers — but sorting by one gives the same order as sorting by the other. That is why production vector databases store embeddings pre-normalised: you get to use fast distance-search hardware and still get cosine ranking. stretch.py makes you prove it rather than believe it.

The identity that connects them

One equation ties the dot product to geometry, and it is worth committing to memory:

a . b  =  |a| × |b| × cos(theta)

In words: the dot product is the product of the two lengths, scaled by how aligned the directions are. Read it three ways, because each one is used somewhere later.

  1. Solve for cos(theta) and you get cosine similarity, which is what you wrote in 1.1. You were not inventing a metric. You were rearranging this.
  2. Set theta to 90°. cos(90°) = 0, so the whole right-hand side is 0. "Dot product of zero means unrelated" is not an analogy — it is this equation.
  3. Set both lengths to 1. The right-hand side becomes just cos(theta), so for unit vectors the dot product already is the cosine, with nothing to divide out.

Two silent failures

Both of these bite on the easiest possible input, and neither raises anything.

arccos of a number just outside its domain is nan. arccos is defined on [-1, 1]. Compute the cosine of a vector with itself and floating point hands you 1.0000000000000002, because the division does not land precisely on 1. Two identical vectors — the easiest case imaginable — is where this bites. One call to np.clip fixes it.

And even clipped, a zero angle is not exactly zero. Ask for the angle between [2,2] and [10,10] and you get 1.2e-06 degrees. That is arccos being ill-conditioned near 0°: since cos(t) ≈ 1 - t²/2, the cosine barely moves as the angle moves, so run backwards the angle moves enormously as the cosine moves. A cosine wrong in its last bit comes back as an angle wrong by about 1e-6 degrees. When you need "do these point the same way?" to full precision, ask it of the cosine, which is well-conditioned there, not of the angle.

== 0 reports False on a textbook right angle. Two vectors perpendicular in exact arithmetic give you 3.06e-17, because 0.1 + 0.2 is not 0.3 and never was. Compare against a tolerance. Build the reflex here, on four lines you can see all of, rather than at 2am inside something you cannot.

Projection: splitting a vector in two

This is the idea with the longest reach in the lesson, and it starts with a picture. Shine a light straight down onto the line through b. The shadow a casts on that line is the projection of a onto b.

The leftover — a minus its projection — is the rejection, and it is always perpendicular to b. That is exercise 5, and it is one line.

Why projection matters more than it looks

"Split a vector into the part explained by a direction, and the part that is not" is one of the most reused moves in the field:

  • Least squares regression finds the projection of your data onto the space your model can reach. The rejection is the residual — your error.
  • PCA (lesson 2.12) finds the directions that leave the smallest rejection. That is all "principal component" means.
  • Attention in a transformer decides how much of one token's vector to mix into another. The amount is a dot product; the mixing is projection.
  • Gram-Schmidt, which you meet near QR decomposition, is literally "project, subtract the projection, repeat".
