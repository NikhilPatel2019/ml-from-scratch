1 and 2 — distance, twice

Distance is magnitude from 1.1 applied to a - b. That is the whole insight, and it is worth pausing on because it is the first time you reuse a function you built rather than a formula you looked up.

The length check in exercise 1 matters for the same reason it mattered in dot_loop: the operation is undefined for mismatched lengths. There is no sensible answer, so failing loudly beats guessing.

For exercise 2, np.linalg.norm(a - b) does it in one call and is what you would reach for in real code. Write it out with square, sum and sqrt at least once — the point of the pair is to see that the vectorised version is the same three operations, composed.

3 — the angle, and the two things that go wrong

Rearranging a·b = |a||b|cos θ for θ gives you arccos of the cosine similarity you already wrote. So the mathematical content of this exercise is one line. Everything else is the numerics, and that is deliberate.

The first failure is nan, and np.clip fixes it. The second is that a zero angle still does not come back as zero, and nothing fixes it — you accommodate it. Read the "two silent failures" section of the lesson again if the reason is not sitting comfortably; why the error is around 1e-6 rather than 1e-16 is the part worth being able to explain.

The general shape, which recurs: an operation can be perfectly correct and still lose you precision, if it is ill-conditioned — if its output moves much faster than its input near the value you care about. Conditioning is a property of the problem, not of your code.

4 and 5 — projection and rejection

The formula is a ratio of two dot products, and the reason it is a ratio is the useful part. a·b tells you how aligned the two are, but it grows if b gets longer. Dividing by b·b removes exactly that growth, which is why doubling b does not move the answer. Then you travel that far along b, which means multiplying by b.

You could also write it as np.dot(a, unit_b) * unit_b using your normalise from 1.1. That is the same arithmetic with the normalisation done once explicitly rather than folded into the ratio, and it is arguably clearer about what is happening. Either is correct; knowing they are the same thing is the point.

Exercise 5 is one line, and if it is more than one line you have not used exercise 4. The property the test checks — that the two pieces add back to a, and that the leftover is perpendicular to b — is not a coincidence to verify, it is the definition. You are splitting a vector into two components along perpendicular directions.

6 — the tolerance

The trap is == 0. The subtler question is what tolerance to pick, and it is worth thinking about for a minute rather than copying 1e-9 because it was in the signature.

Too tight — say 1e-30 — and genuinely perpendicular vectors are reported as not perpendicular, because the rounding error is bigger than your threshold. Too loose — say 0.1 — and vectors that are visibly not perpendicular pass. The default here is a reasonable middle for vectors of modest size. For very large or very small vectors, an absolute tolerance on the dot product is the wrong tool entirely; you would scale it by the magnitudes, or compare the cosine instead. That is what np.isclose's rtol exists for.
