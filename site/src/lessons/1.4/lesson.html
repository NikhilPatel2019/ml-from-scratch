The same numbers, a different question

In 1.3 a matrix was a filing cabinet. X had one row per document and one column per feature, and every operation was about organising numbers.

This lesson takes the same object — the same grid, stored the same way — and asks something else of it. Not what does this matrix hold, but what does this matrix do.

The answer is that a matrix is a function. It takes a vector in and hands a vector back, and the way it does that has a shape you can see. Both readings are used constantly, often on the same page. When the matrix holds your data it is a table; when it multiplies your data it is a transformation. A shape of (3, 2) that meant "three things, two features" yesterday means "takes 2-D in, gives 3-D out" today.

A matrix takes a vector and gives back a vector

Start small enough to check by hand. There are two ways to read M @ v, they always agree, and one of them explains far more than the other.

Where the basis vectors land

The basis vectors are the plainest ones there are: one 1, everything else 0. Run [1, 0] through the column reading and the weights are 1 and 0, so what comes out is column 0, untouched. [0, 1] picks out column 1.

And knowing those two is enough to know where everything goes, because every vector is built from the basis. v = [4, 5] is 4 of the first arrow and 5 of the second, so afterwards it is 4 of wherever the first one went and 5 of wherever the second went: 4×[2, 0] + 5×[−1, 3] = [3, 15]. The same arithmetic as before, read as a sentence.

Exercise 3 has you build this rather than take it from me, and the shape is the interesting part. M is (d_out, d_in), and one image per input axis is (d_in, d_out). Those are not the same shape.

Rows and columns: the convention clash

Here is the practical problem, and it is the one that will cost you real time. Every textbook, every video and every paper writes a transformation as acting on a single column vector, M @ v. Your data, since 1.3, is a table with one thing per row, because that is how records are stored.

What "linear" actually means

"Linear" is not a soft word for "roughly straight". It is two rules, and a function either obeys them for every input or it is not linear: f(a + b) == f(a) + f(b), and f(s×a) == s×f(a). Adding first is the same as adding after; scaling first is the same as scaling after.

If you drew a grid on the plane and applied a matrix to every point, the grid lines would stay straight, stay parallel and stay evenly spaced, and the origin would not move. That is the picture those two rules make, and it is what 3Blue1Brown's chapter 3 animates.

Decide where the basis goes, and the matrix writes itself

Read the rule backwards and it becomes a construction kit. To build a transformation, decide where you want the basis vectors to land and write those down as the columns.

Turning by an angle

Rotation is the same construction, and it needs one fact from trigonometry that is smaller than it looks. Walk the point [1, 0] counter-clockwise around the unit circle by some angle. Where is it? That is the definition of cosine and sine. They are not formulas to memorise; they are the names of the two coordinates of that point.

One after another

You have a transformation A and another one B, and you want the result of doing A and then B. The composition of two linear functions is itself linear — neither step can bend a straight line — and every linear function is a matrix. So a single matrix does the pair, and finding it is exercise 6.

The order is where everyone slips, so use the shapes rather than the story. If the first transformation is (k, d_in) and the second is (d_out, k), only one of the two possible products has the ks meeting in the middle, and it leaves you with (d_out, d_in) — the shape a single transformation from d_in to d_out has to have.

Why this makes neural networks click

A layer of a neural network does exactly two things to the rows it is handed: moves them with a matrix, and adds a bias to every row. That is exercise 7, and the (n, d) in, (n, d_out) out shape is why a network can process a whole batch at once.
