Passing tests is the floor, not the finish. This is the part that decides whether any of it stays with you.

You are done when

Not when the tests pass — when you can answer these out loud, without looking anything up:

  1. M is (3, 2). How many components does its input have, and its output? Which of those two numbers is the row count?
  2. Where in a matrix do you look to find where [0, 1] lands? State it as a rule, not as an answer about one matrix.
  3. Your data X is (100, 3) and M is (5, 3). Write the expression that moves every row, and say what shape comes out. What goes wrong in the other order?
  4. State the two rules that make a function linear. Which one does f(v) = M @ v + c break, and what happens to the origin under it?
  5. Why is a neural network layer affine rather than linear?
  6. Without computing anything: does a rotation change the length of a vector? Answer from the picture rather than the arithmetic.
  7. Give two transformations you can picture where the other order gives a different answer, and say where one specific point ends up in each case.
  8. Ten linear layers with nothing between them are equivalent to how many layers? Say why in one sentence, using the word "composition".
  9. What does tanh between the layers change about that answer?

If one of those makes you hesitate, the fix is not to re-read this page. Open a Python prompt, build a 2×2 matrix, and apply it to [1, 0] and [0, 1]. This lesson is learned by watching where two arrows go.

Write your notes

notes.md in the lesson folder holds the same questions plus one more, asking which bug raised an error and which one handed you plausible numbers. Answer them in your own words — the things I got wrong section will be the most valuable thing in your repository six months from now.

What this sets up

You can move space in any direction you like. Lesson 1.5 asks the opposite question: given where something landed, can you get back to where it started? Sometimes yes, sometimes no — and the cases where you cannot are the ones that explain what "singular", "rank" and "solving Ax = b" actually mean. Exercise 2 of this lesson, where a (1, 2) matrix squashes every row to a single number, is already an example of a transformation nobody can undo.
